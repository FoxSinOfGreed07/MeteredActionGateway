**D1: making the ERP write happen once.** The options:

- **Idempotency key at the ERP:** you send a unique key and the ERP refuses repeats. This relies on the ERP supporting it.
- **Own dedupe table:** the gateway records what it has sent. This relies on the gateway's knowledge being enough, but the gateway can't see what the ERP did after a timeout.

**D2: the outcome is unknown after a timeout.** The options:

- **Retry:** relies on repetition being safe.

**D3: where the wallet debit sits.** The options:

- **Debit first, then act:** refund on failure.
- **Act, then debit:** charge only after success.

**D4: the caching pattern for ERP reads.** The options:

- **Probabilistic early refresh:** entries randomly refresh shortly before they expire.
- **Stale-while-revalidate:** serve the old value while one request refreshes in the background.
- **Request coalescing:** simultaneous requests share one in-flight fetch.

**D7: concurrency control on a tenant's wallet.** The race: the balance is 500, and two holds of 300 each both read 500, both see enough, and both proceed. The balance ends at −100. The options:

- **SQS:** a managed AWS queue.

## Section 4: The eight scenarios

Each scenario needs an automated test **and** a one-line statement of expected behaviour.

**1. Duplicate submit, 200ms apart.** At 200ms, the second request probably arrives while the first is still executing. - Does "identical" mean the same action record, or literally the same response even if the status has changed in between?
- What if the same key arrives with a *different* payload?

**2. SAP commits, then the connection drops.** The required behaviour is spelled out. "Query SAP by reference" means the gateway must attach an identifier that SAP stores and can search by. You pass with exactly one payment in SAP, the action resolved to success after the query, and credits captured, not refunded. Hidden questions:

- What if the query itself times out?
- What if it returns a stale "not found"? That's exactly the review's twist.

**3. Two agents pay the same invoice concurrently.** Two different agents means two *different* idempotency keys, so idempotency doesn't help at all. This tests business-level conflicts on the same object, not request duplication. The loser should get a conflict response showing the invoice's current state, such as "already paid." Open questions:

- Where is the conflict detected: the gateway or the ERP?
- Does the loser's hold get released?
- What about partial payments, where two half-payments might be legitimate?

**4. Wallet holds 500; two 300-credit actions in parallel.** "Second" just means whichever loses the race. "Rejected at hold time" means rejected at admission, before queuing or calling the ERP. "Never driven negative" means not even for a moment. The underlying concept is available balance = balance − active holds.

**5. 429s for 90 seconds during a burst of 5,000 actions.** This is the operations scenario, with four requirements:

- **Back off:** stop hammering SAP while it's returning 429s.
- **Visible queue depth:** expose a metric or endpoint.
- **No action lost:** if your retry cap gives up after, say, 30 seconds, actions die during a 90-second outage. Refusing *new* submissions at the door is backpressure, not loss. Dropping *accepted* actions is loss.
- **Fairness:** a tenant with 100 actions shouldn't wait behind another tenant's 4,900.

Two more things to notice. You won't want an automated test that literally waits 90 seconds, so think about time control. And all 5,000 actions' credits stay held during the outage.

**6. Vendor master changed in SAP via another channel; the webhook arrives 40 seconds late.** This is the trickiest wording in the document. The timeline is: at T, the change happens in SAP. Your cache doesn't know. At T+40s, the webhook arrives.

Read literally, "stale read after the write is impossible to observe" can't be guaranteed: during those 40 seconds you have no way of knowing the change happened. So you have to state your interpretation. The plausible intended meanings are:

- Once the webhook is processed, nothing older can ever be served again. That includes the classic race where an in-flight cache refill writes the old value back *after* your invalidation.
- Duplicate or out-of-order webhooks can't roll the cache backwards.
- The 40-second gap must fall within your declared budget for vendor master data. The 40 seconds was probably chosen deliberately to test that number.

**7. 1,000 agents request the same vendor record at the moment its cache expires.** You pass with one upstream read, with the others served the shared result or a stale value within budget. The wording implies a difference between "expired" and "too old to serve." Hidden questions:

- If you run several gateway processes, is it one read total or one per process?
- What do the 1,000 receive if that single read fails or gets a 429?

**8. Permanent failure after the hold was placed.** You must distinguish permanent errors (validation; don't retry) from transient ones (429, 503, timeout). The ledger must record three facts: the hold, the release, and the reason. There must be no net charge. One unstated question: if the LLM classification already ran, does that cost anything?

## Section 5: The constraints

**Staleness budgets.** Read this carefully: *"No cache value may outlive the write that made it wrong by more than its declared staleness budget."* The clock starts when the write happens in the ERP, not when you hear about it. **LLM determinism at the boundary.** The LLM itself may vary its answers, but your gateway's behaviour must not: the same content returns the same stored answer. **Durable record before the ERP call; crashes must be recoverable.** Consider a crash after the record is written but around the time of the call.
